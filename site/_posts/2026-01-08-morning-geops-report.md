As the second week of January 2026 begins, the international landscape remains dominated by the fallout from the U.S. operation in Venezuela, mounting global tensions, and shifting alliances. New independent analyses shed further light on the underlying motives, regional implications, and the evolving nature of U.S. power projection.

**Venezuela: Motives, Backlash, and the Role of Private Interests**

The U.S. abduction of Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro continues to draw sharp condemnation and intensify global instability. **Jonathan Cook** characterizes the operation as a hallmark of “destabilizing and exploitative corporate capitalism,” arguing that Washington’s actions reflect the dangers of a unipolar world order. The motivations behind the raid are increasingly scrutinized, with **Larry C. Johnson** pointing to billionaire hedge fund manager Paul Singer’s significant financial stakes in Venezuelan debt and oil assets as a possible driver for the Trump administration’s decision. This perspective is echoed by other analysts who note that the operation has paved the way for major Western energy firms, such as Shell and BP, to seek re-entry into Venezuela’s resource sector.

The operation’s legality and broader implications are also under debate. **Richard Falk** warns that the UN’s failure to constrain U.S. actions is eroding the credibility of international law, while **Anatol Lieven** cautions that the aggressive U.S. approach risks alienating neighboring countries and provoking confrontation with global powers like Russia and China. The recent U.S. seizure of a Russian-flagged oil tanker linked to Venezuela, as reported by **Chris Hedges**, has further heightened fears of escalation and drawn strong rebukes from Moscow and Beijing.

**Israel’s Expanding Regional Strategy**

Amid the global distraction over Venezuela, Israel is intensifying both military and diplomatic initiatives. **Chris Hedges** highlights Israel’s recent recognition of Somaliland, interpreting it as part of a broader strategy to extend its regional influence and counter rivals such as Turkey. Concurrently, Israeli operations in Palestine, Syria, and Lebanon continue unabated, marked by repeated ceasefire violations and aggressive maneuvers. These actions are contributing to regional instability and complicating prospects for diplomatic resolution.

**Systemic Shifts: The End of the Postwar Order**

Beyond immediate crises, analysts are drawing attention to deeper systemic changes. **Michael Hudson** emphasizes that the rapid transformation of U.S. national security strategy and the unraveling of the post-World War II economic order are not simply the result of current leadership, but part of a broader phenomenon. The increasing reliance on coercive measures—military, economic, and legal—by the U.S. and its allies is fueling resistance and prompting realignment among adversaries and non-aligned states.

**Trends and Outlook**

The prevailing trend is the normalization of extra-legal interventionism and the blurring of lines between state and corporate interests in foreign policy. The Venezuela crisis has become a flashpoint for great power rivalry, with the risk of broader conflict rising as Russia and China signal their opposition to U.S. actions. Israel’s assertive regional posture, meanwhile, reflects a wider pattern of states exploiting global distractions to advance their strategic agendas.

Looking ahead, analysts warn of a period marked by heightened volatility, the erosion of international norms, and the increasing likelihood of miscalculation. The sidelining of diplomatic channels and the weakening of multilateral institutions are expected to deepen global divides, with energy infrastructure and foreign assets likely to remain key flashpoints in an increasingly unstable environment.
