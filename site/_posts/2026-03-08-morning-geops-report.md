As the Iran war grinds into its third week, the strategic and political consequences are becoming more pronounced, with new developments highlighting both the operational strain on U.S. forces and the enduring resilience of Iran. The conflict’s ripple effects are also being felt in global naval operations and the international arms industry.

**Naval Power and Submarine Crisis**

The U.S. Navy’s undersea capabilities are facing a critical juncture. **Daniel L. Davis** reports that the Ohio-class SSGN submarines—once the backbone of U.S. conventional strike power—are set for decommissioning starting this year, marking “the end of an era for these powerful vessels that have significantly influenced U.S. military operations.” With hundreds of Tomahawk missiles already expended in the opening phases of Operation Epic Fury, the looming retirement of these submarines raises concerns about the Navy’s future strike capacity, especially as tensions with China persist. Compounding the issue, the Los Angeles-class attack submarines, which now represent nearly half the U.S. attack fleet, remain in service due to delays in transitioning to the Virginia-class, reflecting what Davis calls a “submarine shortage” at a time of rising competition from China’s expanding undersea fleet.

**Air Power: Upgrades and Strategic Lessons**

The air campaign over Iran has showcased the effectiveness of U.S. fifth-generation fighters. **Dr. Brent M. Eastwood** highlights the $11 billion “Super Raptor” upgrade for the F-22, which has enhanced its capabilities and proven its worth in recent operations. However, analysts caution that the success of stealth-led bombing against Iran may not be replicable against more advanced adversaries. As Davis notes, “China won’t be so easy to strike in a war,” underscoring the unique vulnerabilities and limitations exposed by the Iran conflict.

**Political and Strategic Fallout**

Despite significant military blows to Iran’s infrastructure, the broader political objectives remain elusive. **Ambassador John R. Bolton** concedes that while Operation Epic Fury has successfully targeted the IRGC, “the political strategy behind the operation is struggling,” with regime change or lasting strategic gains still out of reach. Meanwhile, **Larry C. Johnson** argues that the Israeli-American attack on Iran is “unjustified and has failed,” asserting that Iran will endure and that ambitions for a Greater Israel are now unrealistic. This view is echoed by a growing chorus of analysts who see the war as a defining, but ultimately unsuccessful, turning point in 21st-century geopolitics.

**Trends and Outlook**

- The U.S. Navy’s strike and undersea capabilities are entering a period of vulnerability, with aging platforms and munitions shortages raising questions about readiness for future conflicts, particularly with China.
- The effectiveness of U.S. air power is being reassessed, with upgrades like the Super Raptor providing short-term advantages but not fundamentally altering the strategic calculus against peer competitors.
- The majority view among independent analysts is that Iran’s regime will survive the current onslaught, and that the war has failed to achieve its broader political aims.
- Calls for a diplomatic resolution are intensifying, but entrenched positions in Washington and Tel Aviv, coupled with Iranian resilience, suggest further escalation and instability in the near term.

In summary, the latest developments underscore the limits of military power in achieving strategic objectives, the mounting strain on U.S. naval and air capabilities, and the enduring unpredictability of the conflict’s outcome. The coming days will be critical in determining whether the U.S. and its allies can adapt to these challenges or face deeper strategic setbacks.
