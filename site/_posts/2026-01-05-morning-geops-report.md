As the week begins, the global geopolitical climate remains tense, with the aftermath of the U.S. operation in Venezuela, debates over the Monroe Doctrine, and the deepening crisis in U.S. foreign policy dominating independent analysis. New commentary focuses on the legal and ideological justifications for American interventionism, the implications for international order, and the growing backlash across Latin America.

**Venezuela: Monroe Doctrine Controversy and U.S. Hubris**

The U.S. abduction of Venezuelan President Nicolás Maduro continues to spark outrage and debate, particularly over the Trump administration’s invocation of the Monroe Doctrine as legal cover. **Larry C. Johnson** argues that “President Monroe’s 1823 speech... does not support Donald Trump’s actions regarding Venezuela,” highlighting a widespread misinterpretation of the doctrine as a license for U.S. domination in the hemisphere. **Caitlin Johnstone** similarly contends that right-wing supporters are “misinterpreting the Monroe Doctrine as justification for such actions,” and frames the intervention as an assault on Venezuelan sovereignty and a blatant attempt to seize resources.

The new U.S. National Security Strategy, as described by **Jeffrey Sachs**, openly promotes “a dangerous and hubristic view of U.S. supremacy and coercion,” which analysts warn is undermining international law and alienating allies. The consensus is that this approach risks setting a precedent for further interventions, with neighboring countries on high alert following explicit threats from Washington.

**Latin American Backlash and Regional Instability**

Across Latin America, the U.S. operation is galvanizing opposition to perceived imperial overreach. Regional leaders and civil society groups are denouncing the intervention, warning of a return to Cold War-era power politics. Analysts note that the Trump administration’s rhetoric and actions are fueling anti-American sentiment and could destabilize the region further if threats against Cuba, Mexico, and Colombia are acted upon.

**Iran: Succession Risks Amid Protests**

While protests in Iran continue, new analysis from **Daniel L. Davis** suggests that a “smooth democratic transition is improbable due to the regime’s strong loyalist base and patronage networks.” The most destabilizing scenario, he writes, would be a “succession collapse among rival factions rather than meaningful reform.” This assessment aligns with the broader view that external actors may seek to exploit unrest as a strategic weapon, but that the regime’s internal cohesion remains a significant barrier to regime change.

**Trends and Outlook**

The dominant trend is the U.S. government’s increasing reliance on coercive power and legal reinterpretation to justify intervention, particularly in Latin America. The majority of analysts agree that this strategy is eroding the foundations of international law and could provoke wider instability, both regionally and globally. In Iran, the risk of internal collapse is seen as more likely than democratization, with external narratives serving as potential pretexts for future intervention.

Looking ahead, independent observers predict heightened volatility in the Western Hemisphere, with the potential for new U.S. interventions and deepening polarization across Latin America. The erosion of diplomatic norms and the rise of overt power politics are expected to define the coming months, raising the risk of broader conflict and long-term damage to the international order.
