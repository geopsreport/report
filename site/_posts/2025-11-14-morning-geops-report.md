As global tensions persist, the latest analyses reveal new developments in U.S. policy toward Palestine, Western media dynamics, and the strategic contest over Central Asian resources—each highlighting the interplay of power, narrative, and resource competition in shaping the international order.

**U.S. Moves to Replace British Mandate in Palestine**

A significant shift in U.S. policy is underway as the Trump administration pushes a UN Security Council resolution that would, according to **Jeffrey Sachs**, “effectively replace British control in Palestine with U.S. authority.” Sachs warns that this initiative, presented as a peace process, is widely seen by analysts as a means to undermine Palestinian sovereignty and entrench Israeli dominance. The move comes amid ongoing violence and allegations of genocide in Gaza, raising concerns that Washington is abandoning any pretense of mediation in favor of direct imperial management. The majority view is that this policy will deepen regional instability and further alienate the U.S. from international consensus on Palestinian self-determination.

**Western Media’s Selective Focus: The Epstein-Israel Connection**

Media coverage of intelligence scandals remains a contentious issue. **Caitlin Johnstone** criticizes the Western press for spotlighting Jeffrey Epstein’s ties to Donald Trump while “neglecting more significant links to Israeli intelligence.” Johnstone argues that this selective reporting is not accidental but reflects a broader pattern: “The Western press only focuses on stories that serve the interests of the US empire, even when more consequential facts are available.” This bias, analysts suggest, distorts public understanding of intelligence operations and shields key geopolitical relationships from scrutiny, contributing to a less informed and more polarized public discourse.

**Central Asia: U.S. Eyes Kazakhstan’s Rare Earths**

In Central Asia, the U.S. is intensifying its focus on Kazakhstan’s rare earth minerals, particularly tungsten, amid rising global competition for strategic resources. **Michael Hudson** reports that the new U.S. ambassador has called for “rapid resource extraction and export,” signaling a push to secure supply chains critical to advanced technologies and defense industries. This move is viewed as part of a broader neo-imperial contest, with the U.S. seeking to counterbalance Russian and Chinese influence in the region. Analysts caution that such strategies risk exacerbating local instability and environmental degradation, while fueling geopolitical rivalries.

**Trends and Outlook**

The dominant trend across these developments is the hardening of great power competition—whether through direct control of conflict zones, manipulation of media narratives, or the scramble for strategic resources. The U.S. is seen as increasingly willing to bypass multilateral norms in pursuit of its interests, both in the Middle East and Central Asia. Meanwhile, the narrowing of public debate and the marginalization of inconvenient facts in Western media further complicate prospects for accountability and informed policy.

Looking ahead, analysts expect these patterns to persist, with rising risks of regional backlash, diplomatic isolation, and intensified resource-driven rivalries. Absent a shift toward genuine diplomacy and media transparency, the outlook remains one of deepening contestation and instability.
