As the global order continues to fragment, the past 12 hours have brought new developments in U.S.-Iran diplomacy, the evolving Pacific airpower balance, and shifting defense procurement strategies in Canada and Europe.

**U.S.-Iran Talks: Mixed Progress Amid Regional Tensions**

The first high-level U.S.-Iran talks since the June 2025 strikes on Iranian nuclear facilities took place in Oman on February 6, 2026. According to **Larry C. Johnson**, these indirect negotiations, mediated by Oman’s Foreign Minister, were nearly derailed over agenda disputes but resumed after the U.S. agreed to Iran’s demand to focus exclusively on nuclear issues—a move Johnson calls “a significant victory for Iran.” The talks occur against a backdrop of heightened military posturing, with the U.S. bolstering its regional presence and Russia expressing outrage over a recent terrorist attack in Moscow, further complicating the security environment.

**Pacific Airpower: F-15EX and F-35s Train Near China**

In a notable demonstration of allied airpower, the U.S. deployed F-15EX Eagle II fighters alongside F-35s in the Pacific for the Resolute Force exercise, marking the F-15EX’s first overseas deployment. **Daniel L. Davis** reports that this pairing is designed to “enhance airpower” and provide a high-end 4.5-generation complement to stealth fighters in the region, signaling a robust U.S. commitment to countering China’s growing military capabilities. Meanwhile, China’s J-35A stealth fighter continues to draw scrutiny for its advanced first-detection and first-strike features, which **Davis** notes “pose a significant challenge to the U.S. Air Force’s F-22 and F-35 fighters.”

**Canada’s Fighter Jet Procurement: Gripen Gains Ground**

Canada’s evolving fighter jet procurement strategy is drawing attention as the government reconsiders its commitment to the F-35. **Daniel L. Davis** highlights that the Saab JAS 39 Gripen E, with its advanced survivability and sensor suite, is now seen as a formidable alternative, offering “cost benefits, increased sovereignty, and industrial advantages.” Canada’s abandonment of the Eurofighter Typhoon, as Davis notes, reflects a desire for deeper integration with U.S. air and missile defense networks, but also a growing debate over balancing sovereignty with interoperability.

**Global Power Shifts and Outlook**

The broader context is one of accelerating multipolarity. **Davis** identifies eight major powers—India, Japan, Brazil, Saudi Arabia, China, the United States, Russia, and the EU—as key players shaping the coming years, moving beyond the traditional U.S.-China-Russia triad. The erosion of arms control frameworks, as well as the proliferation of advanced military technologies, point to a more unpredictable and competitive global environment.

Looking ahead, the outcome of U.S.-Iran talks remains uncertain, with the potential for either de-escalation or renewed confrontation. In the Pacific, the integration of advanced fighter platforms signals a continued arms race, while Canada’s procurement decisions may influence allied airpower and defense industrial trends across NATO. The prevailing trend is toward greater fragmentation, technological competition, and the recalibration of alliances in response to shifting geopolitical realities.
