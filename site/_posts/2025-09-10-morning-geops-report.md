As the international order continues to fragment, the past 12 hours have seen significant new developments in the Gaza crisis, US domestic and foreign policy, and the evolving discourse around historical memory and media coverage.

**Gaza: Surrender Unlikely to End Violence, International Pressure Mounts**

Despite mounting international condemnation of Israel’s military campaign in Gaza, analysts warn that a Hamas surrender would not end the violence against Palestinians. **Caitlin Johnstone** argues that “even if Hamas were to surrender, it would not end the violence against Palestinians, but rather lead to their permanent erasure from their own territory.” This perspective reflects a growing consensus among independent observers that the Israeli government’s objectives extend beyond defeating Hamas, raising further concerns about the long-term future of the Palestinian population. Meanwhile, Spain has announced a comprehensive arms embargo and additional measures to pressure Israel, as reported by **Chris Hedges**, signaling a deepening rift between some European states and traditional US policy.

**US Policy Shift: Focus Turns to Latin America, Immigration Raids Strain Alliances**

In a notable shift, US military planners are proposing to pivot their strategic focus from China and Russia to perceived threats in Latin America and the Caribbean. **Chris Hedges** highlights that this change is outlined in a draft of the new National Defense Strategy and coincides with President Trump’s executive order to rename the Department of Defense as the “War Department.” At the same time, the largest-ever immigration raid at a Hyundai plant in Georgia—resulting in the arrest of nearly 500 mostly Korean workers—has drawn sharp criticism from South Korean officials and raised questions about the future of US-South Korea relations amid ongoing economic investments.

**Weaponization of Historical Memory and Media Narratives**

The discourse around the Holocaust and its use in contemporary political debates has come under renewed scrutiny. In an interview with **Chris Hedges**, historian Raz Segal contends that the Holocaust has been “weaponized by Zionists and the Israeli government to promote a narrative that distorts historical realities,” emphasizing exceptionalism over genuine education. This manipulation of historical memory, coupled with ongoing criticism of Western media’s reporting on Gaza—such as the **New York Times**’s failure to accurately distinguish civilian from combatant deaths—underscores the broader information war shaping public perceptions of the conflict.

**Trends and Outlook**

The dominant trends point to intensifying international pressure on Israel, a reorientation of US strategic priorities toward the Western Hemisphere, and growing skepticism about the objectivity of historical and media narratives. Analysts expect continued diplomatic fallout as European states adopt more assertive measures against Israel, while US domestic and foreign policy shifts may generate new tensions with key allies. The ongoing manipulation of historical memory and media coverage is likely to further polarize public opinion and complicate efforts to resolve the underlying crises. The overall trajectory remains one of deepening fragmentation and volatility in the global order.
